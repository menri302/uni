# 1.1 The Types of Firms

> [!abstract] Overview
> The legal form determines **who** owns the company, **who** is liable, **who** manages it and **how** capital can be raised.
> Distinction: **Partnerships** and **Corporations** (Kapitalgesellschaften).

## Why is it like this?

**Liability**
- Unlimited → private assets are liable → capital comes more from private sources or bank loans (baker: the bank demands collateral, e.g. the private house)
- Limited → only company assets are liable → attractive for investors (€1,000 in a stock corporation = maximum loss of €1,000)
  - If you become a partner, you are jointly and severally liable for the full amount (initially 100%, regardless of your share) → you must recover the excess internally from the other partners

**Minimum capital**
- **Creditor protection** with limited liability → minimum capital (GmbH: €25,000) – in insolvency, creditors are paid from this

**Transferability of shares**
- Personal partners → sale to outsiders requires consent → no market (OHG)
- Freely tradable shares → stock exchange, capital increases → many small investors (AG/SE)

**Bonds** → creditors don't know the management → annual financial statements and a rating are needed → only large corporations (corporation: bond, craft business: bank loan)

**Silent partners** → money without a say (a friend gives €10,000 for my sister's café and receives a share of the profit)

> [!tip] Key rule
> The **more limited the liability** and the **more freely shares are tradable**, the **easier** it is for a company to raise **equity** and **debt on the capital market** (bonds). The more personal the liability, the more financing stays with owners and bank loans.

## Equity vs. Debt

|                                | **Equity (Eigenkapital)**                         | **Debt (Fremdkapital)**                                             |
| ------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Ownership**                  | Yes                                               | No                                                                  |
| **Voting rights**              | Yes (exception: preferred shares as mezzanine)    | No (exception: in insolvency)                                       |
| **Term**                       | No term                                           | Short-, medium- or long-term                                        |
| **Repayment**                  | No                                                | Yes                                                                 |
| **Returns to holders**         | Residual claim (dividends)                        | Fixed claim (interest, repayment of principal)                      |
| **Tradability**                | See previous slide                                | Bank loans difficult, bonds easier (if listed)                      |
| **Collateralization**          | No                                                | Possible                                                            |
| **Rank in case of insolvency** | Last                                              | In general: senior (unless subordinated)                            |

> [!note] Note
> Mezzanine capital (e.g. profit-participation rights, convertible bonds, preferred shares) often lies between equity and debt.

# 1.2 Ownership Versus Control of Corporations

## Riskiness and Voting rights
**Equity holders have voting rights, while dept holders have not, why?**
--> Equity holders's earnings are dependent on a companies earnings while debt holders's aren't (equity holders earnings are based of the earnings after all liabilities)
--> Equity holders hold more risk so they decide

## Ownership vs. Control in a Stock Company
- Shareholders liability is capped to their respective investments
- Shareholders cannot terminate, but sell their shares on the secondary market (stock market)

**A stock companies (AG/SE) equity capital is split into many small parts (stocks)**
--> potentially large investor base 
--> direct control no longer works 
--> **indirect control**

- Shareholders meet in an annual **general assembly (HV)** - one vote per share
- Supervisory board controls the management board
  --> Appointing the MB
  --> Define compensation
  --> Responsible for corporations audit (not doing it themselves, just responsible for it)

## The Financial Manager

**Three main tasks**

| Task                     | What it means                                                                                                                                                                    | Why it matters                                                        |
| ------------------------ | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Cash management**      | Manage working capital, which is needed in the short term to run the business → ensure enough cash to meet day-to-day obligations                                                | Seems simple, but can make the difference between success and failure |
| **Financing decisions**  | Large investments need additional funds → raise money from existing and new owners by selling more shares (**equity**), or borrow through a loan or a bond (**debt**)            | Determines the capital structure of the firm                          |
| **Investment decisions** | Investments usually come with (initial) cash outflows → weigh costs and benefits of all investments and projects → decide which ones are a good use of the shareholders' capital | Fundamentally shapes what the firm does and whether it adds value     |

## The Goal of the Firm

- **Sole proprietorship:** firm's goal = owner's goal
- **Multiple owners:**  all owners prefer decisions which increase their shares value (in partnerships it may differ)

| Approach | Who | Goal | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Shareholder-value approach** | Shareholders (owners) | Maximize firm value; goals of other stakeholders play a subordinate role | Increase value of shares |
| **Stakeholder approach** | All groups that have an interest in the company and can affect or be affected by its operations | Consider the interests of all stakeholders | **Employees:** fair compensation, work safety<br>**Environment:** resource efficiency, air pollution<br>**Society:** engagement in local communities, paying taxes |

## The Firm and Society

**Does society benefit from decisions that increase the firm's value of equity?**

| Answer  | Argument                                                                                                 | Example                                                                                                                                                 |
| ------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **No**  | Increasing the **value of equity** might come at the **expense of others**                               | Production process or product itself **pollutes the environment**<br><br>**2008 financial crisis:** led to a crisis that harmed the **broader economy** |
| **Yes** | The decision might create a product that **benefits society**                                            | Investment in the development of **cancer medication**                                                                                                  |
| **Yes** | Even if it does not benefit society directly, increasing the **value of equity** is **good for society** | –                                                                                                                                                       |

> [!tip] Conclusion
> **Appropriate public policy and regulation** are required to ensure that **corporate and societal interests** remain aligned.

**Problem**: Corporation are run by a management team that is separate from its owners
→ possible conflicts of interest

### Agency problems
Agency problems result from **information asymmetries** and cause **inefficiencies**.

**Managers vs. owners**
  - Managers have little incentive to work in the stakeholders' interest if this goes against their **self-interest**
  - Example: maximizing their own **salary**, **empire building**, **perks**

**Stakeholders vs. stakeholders**
  - Some stakeholders benefit from the managers' decisions, others lose
  - Example: **employees** and **society** benefit, **shareholders** lose

### Consequences and solutions

| Solution | Explanation |
|---|---|
| **Incentive-compatible compensation** | Pay managers in a way that aligns their interests with those of shareholders |
| **Threat of replacement** | Current shareholders or new shareholders can replace managers |
| **Hostile takeover** | New shareholders buy the company when the **stock price drops "too much"** and replace management |
**Corporate bankruptcy** can be seen as a **change in ownership**, not necessarily as a **failure of the underlying business**.

# 1.3 The Stock Market

## Stock Markets
Shares of public companies trade at a **stock market** (exchange; secondary market)
**Examples**: Euronext (largest in Europe), NYSE and Nasdaq (US)

**Markets provide liquidity**

> [!NOTE] Liquidity
> An asset is **liquid** if it can be sold **quickly and easily** at a price that is **very close to the price at which it could be purchased** at the same time
> → the **bid-ask spread** indicates liquidity: the **more illiquid** the asset, the **higher the spread**

- Liquidity is attractive to investors → makes **timing and duration** of an investment **flexible**
- High liquidity typically requires some **standardization**

| High liquidity                 | Low liquidity                                     |
| ------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------- |
| Apple, Intel, Tesla (stocks)   | small stocks, e.g. First Bancshares, Daxor, Moog  |

**Markets determine a market price** for the company's shares
- Prices follow from **supply and demand**
- Share prices give **constant feedback about the managers' performance** (but also reflect risk factors of the investment)

## Primary and Secondary Markets

|                    | **Primary Market**                                                                                                    | **Secondary Market**                                                                                     |
| ------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **What is traded** | **Newly issued shares** of a company                                                                                  | Shares **between investors**, **without the company's involvement**                                      |
| **Frequency**      | Companies issue new shares only **occasionally** → small fraction of all trades                                       | **Large fraction** of all trades                                                                         |
| **Process**        | **IPO** (Initial Public Offering), <br>**SPO** (Secondary Public Offering)<br>typically organized by investment banks | Order placed at a **stock exchange**, shares bought from a person or institution that already holds them |
| **Issuer**         | **Company** that sells new shares                                                                                     |                                                                                                          |

## Pricing at Stock Markets

Market participants can submit **limit orders** and **market orders**.

> [!NOTE] Market order vs. limit order
> **Market order**: buy/sell order **without a price limit** → executed at the current market price
> - **billigst** = market **buy** order
> - **bestens** = market **sell** order
>
> **Limit order**: buy/sell order **with a price limit**
> - buy limit = **maximum** price · sell limit = **minimum** price
>
> → in an auction, market orders are **executable at every price** → counted in demand (billigst) / supply (bestens) at **every** possible price
> → executed **first**, but only fully if there is enough volume on the other side

| Mechanism              | What happens                                                                                                                                           | Key point                                                                                                          |
| ---------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **Auction**            | In Xetra: **one** (midday) or **three** (opening, midday, closing) auctions a day. Orders are collected in the **order book**, which bundles liquidity | The price is set so that the **highest possible volume** is transacted, and **all executable orders** are executed |
| **Continuous trading** | Orders are collected in the **central order book**                                                                                                     | **Each executable order** is executed immediately                                                                  |
| **Market makers**      | Set **bid** and **ask prices** at which they are willing to buy or sell stocks                                                                         | Ensure **market liquidity** for specific assets. Compensation: the **bid-ask spread**                              |

![[Pasted image 20261017143935.png]]

> [!NOTE] Auction price: principle of maximum executable volume (Meistausführungsprinzip)
> 1. All buy and sell orders (limit + market orders) are collected in the order book
> 2. For every possible price: **demand** = all buyers with limit ≥ price, **supply** = all sellers with limit ≤ price → executable volume = min(demand, supply)
> 3. The auction price is the price with the **highest executable volume**
> 4. **All** executable orders are filled at this **single price**
>
> | Price | Demand (cum.) | Supply (cum.) | Executable |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | 99 € | 500 | 100 | 100 |
> | **100 €** | **400** | **350** | **350** ← max |
> | 101 € | 200 | 450 | 200 |
>
> → auction price **100 €**, 350 shares traded
## Market Indices

- **Goal:** track the dynamics of a group of assets in a standardized way
- **Differences:** stocks, bonds, commodities; size of firms, sustainability; national, international


A **market index** reports the **value and performance** of a particular selection of securities.

**Consideration of dividents**

| Type                                                         | How it works                                                               | Example                                                                          |
| ------------------------------------------------------------ | -------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Price index** (Preisindex)                                 | Index level is determined **exclusively** on the basis of **share prices** | –                                                                                |
| **Total return index** (Performanceindex)                    | Considers **cash dividends** and other income from owning the shares       | –                                                                                |
**Method of calculation**

| **Price-weighted index** (Preisgewichteter Index)            | Weights: **average share prices** of index members  | **Dow Jones Industrial Average** (DJIA, 30 large industrial U.S. stocks)         |
| ------------------------------------------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Value-weighted index** (Kapitalisierungsgewichteter Index) | Weights: **market capitalization** of index members | **S&P 500** (500 largest listed U.S. stocks), **DAX 40** (Deutscher Aktienindex) |

# Fintech

# 1.4 Fintech: Finance and Technology

> [!abstract] Definition
> **FinTech**: **new technologies** in finance.
> The financial industry: **early adopter** of technological innovation (for example the first stock ticker system or ATMs)

| Area | What it does | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Security and verification** | Records a transaction in a **publicly verifiable** way, **without an intermediary** that certifies authenticity | **Blockchain** |
| **Automation of banking services** | Replaces manual processes with software | **Payment apps**, **robo-advisors** |
| **Big data and machine learning** | Helps financial service companies **target their products** to consumers better | Personalized offers based on customer data |

> [!warning] Competition
> FinTech has led to **new competition** in all areas of financial services. 
> **Example**: **Start-ups** and **tech giants** like Apple and Google offer payment services that banks used to provide alone.
> → may **ultimately reshape the financial industry**.
